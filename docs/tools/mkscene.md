title: mkscene

`mkscene` cooks a scene, written as JSON by the playground's scene editor or by hand, into a DAT, the file a program reads off its disc and draws with [`scene`](scene), laid out as [`format/scene`](format/scene) describes.

[`yaroze`](yaroze.md) has it as `yaroze scene`, with the same flags.

```bash
$ mkscene -o assets/CUBES.DAT scenes/cubes.json
created "assets/CUBES.DAT": 232 bytes, 1 object, 0 sprites, 1 light, camera at 0,0,-1000 looking at 0,0,0
  cube: CUBE.TMD object 0 at 0,0,0
```

The JSON names a camera (where it is and what it looks at, with a twist), the projection distance, the ambient light, up to three lights, the TIMs and TMDs the scene needs by their paths on the disc, the objects placed from those models, each with a name, a position, a rotation (4096 a whole turn), a scale (4096 as 1.0) and two fields, numbers of the program's own that `ObjectFields` returns (a kind, a score, a door's key), the sprites cut from those textures with the fields of `GsSPRITE`, and up to three tile layers, the maps of a 2D game. The program registers the same TIMs and TMDs with package `disc`, or packs them into an archive with [`mkarc`](mkarc.md), loads them with the scene, and `scene.Load` binds each to its file by that path through a `Source`: `scene.Files` for the disc's own files, `scene.NewArchive` for an archive's entries (`ASSETS/CUBE.TIM` finding the entry `CUBE.TIM`, a packed one unpacked once and kept), `scene.Sources` for both. It sends the textures to video memory, maps the models and places everything, so that `Draw` draws it each frame and `Object(name)` finds the objects the program moves.

A tile layer is a texture cut into 16 by 16 cells in reading order and a map of indexes into them, 4,096 cells at most (273 across a level a screen high, or 64 by 64), with 0xffff for an empty cell, a scroll, a speed and a brightness, drawn behind everything by `GsSortFixBg16` as the [tiles](../examples/tiles.md) example draws its own, the first layer farthest. Each has a name, which `Layer(name)` finds it by, and flags for each cell of its texture: `Solid`, `OneWay`, `Hurts` and `Pickup`, which mean what the program makes them mean, and four bits of the program's own. `Cell` and `Flags` say what is at a column and row of the map and `CellAt` and `FlagsAt` at a pixel, so that a program asks the map whether a box would go into a wall instead of keeping a copy of the level, and `SetCell` changes a cell, as when a coin is taken. `Scroll(x, y)` scrolls every layer, each its speed's percent of the way, so that a far layer at 50 moves half as far as the ground at 100. A file from before layers, with one `background`, reads as a layer named `background` at speed 100. In the playground, Add, Tile layer cuts a layer from a TIM, and its row in the sidebar opens its map in the picture's place, painted with the pen, fill, rectangle and eraser in the toolbar and zoomed with the wheel. The inspector holds its name and texture, the palette of cells, the flags of the cells picked, which the palette and the map mark (Flags in the toolbar hides them on the map), and its map's size, scroll, speed, brightness and place among the layers. The [jumpcube](../examples/jumpcube.md) example's level is made so: hills far off at half speed, and a ground of flagged cells that its cube runs and jumps on.

A scene can instead be a room: `"room"` holds a map of cells for the [`stage`](stage) package, which draws rooms of tiles with heights as Dragon Quest VII drew its towns. Its `cells` are rows of runes in stage's legend (`.` floor, `#` wall, `r` rock, `c` a cabin's wall, `b` and `p` a bed, `t` a chest, `~` water, `o` a pot, `x` blocked ground, a digit a doorway), `heights` the ground's level at each cell as a digit, `floor` and `wall` indexes into the program's textures, `tint`, `sky` and `background` colors, `rough`, `black`, `rockTall`, `rockLean`, `camUp` and `camBack` as stage and the program take them, `links` from each doorway digit to the scene it leads to, and `markers`, cells named for the program's own people and things, each with two fields. A room's file leaves the camera and the lights to the program, whose own camera draws it: `scene.Load` keeps the map's rows as strings over the file's own bytes, `Room` hands them to a `stage.Setting`, and `Link` and `Marker` answer where a doorway leads and where a marker stands. The [quest](../examples/quest.md) example's three rooms, a hall, a meadow and the house in it, are such files under `scenes/`, which the playground opens as a project to paint them live. In the playground, New room under the Scenes sidebar's + starts a room, and the Scenes layout paints it: a pen, a height brush, a fill, a rectangle and markers in the toolbar, with a look tool under View, in the picture, which draws the room as the console does, turned an eighth at a time with `[` and `]`, or on its map from above in the picture's corner; the inspector beside it holds the brushes and the doorways, and sets the room's look, which TIMs the editor draws its floor and walls with (an `art` key of the editor's own, which `mkscene` leaves), where each doorway leads, and the markers' names and fields.

`mkscene -r CUBES.DAT` prints a cooked scene as JSON. A project holds as many scenes as it likes, each cooked by a line of its own, and the program loads whichever it is on, as the example does on Start. In the playground, while the program runs, each change to the scene is also written into the program's memory over the file it loaded, and `scene` reads it again at the next frame, so the console follows the editor without a rebuild; a program that draws a room through its own `stage.Setting` rather than `Draw` calls `Reload` itself each frame and places the room again when it says so, and the toolbar's Play from here (P) asks it, through `Teleport`, to put its figure on the cell the editor's camera looks at, in that room: a program shown another room loads the one asked for, as it would on a doorway, and the editor then writes the room as painted over it. A file grows as it is painted, so a program that wants its rooms edited live reads them into memory with room to spare (`disc.File.Into`), as the quest example does its rooms and jumpcube its level. The [scene](../examples/scene.md) example is the square example's cube placed by two scene files.
